Abstract—  Electronic nose  calibration usually relies on controlled gas exposures with known concentrations. In 
practice, however, unlabeled responses are much easier to 
collect because they carry no concentration annotation.  The 
present experiments reuse controlled exposures with their 
concentration labels withheld during pretraining, so they 
demonstrate reduced labeled -calibration requirements rather 
than reduced gas-generation cost. This study explores whether 
those unlabeled measurements can be useful before the 
calibration stage through sensor-aware self-supervised learning. 
The proposed model uses a shared dilated one -dimensional 
convolutional encoder to learn temporal behavior. Sensor embeddings preserve channel identity, while attention 
captures relationships across the array. During pretraining, the model reconstructs masked time spans and complete 
sensor channels. The resulting encoder is then adapted using a limited number of concentration labels. The evaluation 
includes a private 20-channel NH3/H2 array, a public 16-channel CO/ethylene array, and a public 36-month sensor drift 
dataset. At the full label budget, transfer achieves mean absolute errors of 14.92 ppm for NH3, 29.16 ppm for H2, 46.16 
ppm for CO, and 1.371 ppm for ethylene. These results correspond to gains of 19.2%, 20.3%, 29.0%, and 31.2% over 
scratch training. The benefit generally becomes larger when fewer labels are available. On sensor drift dataset, oracle-
class gas-conditional concentration error decreases in three future partitions by 17.3%, 29.9%, and 2.8%. Quantitative 
transfer therefore retains some within -gas concentration information under long -term drift, although the benefit 
decreases under the strongest future shift.

label-efficient calibration, sensor attention.

II. RELATED WORK 
Quantitative estimation becomes important when an 
Electronic nose is used as a measuring instrument rather than 
only as a recognizer. Fonollosa et al. [8] used reservoir 
computing to estimate rapidly changing concentrations from 
slower sensor responses. Their study drew attention to transient 
dynamics, which may be lost when a response is reduced to a 
steady-state value. 
Deep models have since combined recognition and 
regression in several ways. Ni et al. [9] used GAF -MTF 
representations with a CNN -LSTM model, reporting 95.31% 
classification accuracy and an R^2 of 0.9510. Tang et al. [10] 
instead paired a single MEMS senso r with a lightweight 
transferred network. Work on smaller datasets includes 
parameter-feature transfer [11], while OT-DARN uses teacher-
student domain adaptation with selected pseudo-labels [12]. 
M-Training [1] and M-S4VMs [2] bring unlabeled samples 
into Electronic nose recognition through predicted labels. This 
strategy can be helpful when labels are scarce, although 
incorrect predictions may be reinforced as training continues. 
Self-supervised learning avoids that particular dependence 
by taking its pretraining target from the measurements 
themselves. Common time -series objectives include masking, 
reconstruction, contrastive learning, and adversarial learning 
[13]. Masked reconst ruction is a natural fit for sensor arrays 
because missing intervals require temporal context. Likewise, 
missing channels encourage the model to use information 
shared across sensors. CEGNN [6] takes a different route by 
combining contrastive learning, gra ph reasoning, and a small 
reference set. 
 In addition to accurately estimating gas concentrations , 
sensor drift due to aging is also a challenging problem. Sensor 
drift gradually changes the response distribution, so models 
trained at one time may not behave in the same way later. 
TDACNN [3] addresses source -target differences, whereas 
SAD-CNN [5] com bines a one -dimensional CNN with 
adversarial adaptation and pseudo -labeling. MDCN [4] uses 
metric learning with a small reference set. Other studies extend 
the setting through contrastive graph reasoning [6] or model -
agnostic meta -learning [7], including t ests involving class 
asymmetry, different devices, and future drift. Most evaluations 
on the 36 -month benchmark emphasize whether gas identity 
survives the shift, while concentration behavior receives less 
attention. For this reason, we use its future partitions to study 
oracle-class gas -conditional concentration regression under 
temporal drift. 
Overall, these studies leave unlabeled representation learning 
and quantitative calibration only partly connected. The present 
study bridges that gap by first learning sensor-specific temporal 
and cross -channel structure without concentration labels, and 
then reu sing the encoder with a small labeled calibration set. 
The future -partition evaluation further examines whether this 
quantitative transfer remains useful as the sensors drift, and 
where further adaptation is needed. The proposed approach is 
expected to red uce the labeled -calibration effort for electronic 
nose systems, and the drift evaluation tests how far the self -
supervised representation stays useful as the sensors age.

Mean pooling captures the overall response level, while 
maximum pooling keeps stronger local activation. The shared 
encoder also limits the number of sensor -specific parameters. 
Thanks to shared weights, the model can learn common 
response dynamics from al l channels rather than fitting a 
separate temporal model for each sensor. 
The implemented temporal encoder receives two channels 
per sensor: the scaled signal and its binary observation mask. It 
applies three convolutions with channel widths 2 → 24 →
32 → 32, kernel sizes (5,5,3), and dilations (1,2,4), 
respectively. Padding preserves the 64 -sample length, and 
GELU follows each convolution. Mean and maximum pooling 
produce 64 features, which are projected through a GELU -
activated linear layer to a 32-dimensional sensor token. 
C. Physical Sensor Identity Encoding 
Sharing the temporal network introduces a possible 
ambiguity. Two different physical sensors may produce 
temporal features with a similar numerical pattern, even though 
their selectivity is different. We therefore attach a learned 
identity embedding 𝑒𝑠 to each sensor token, 
𝑧𝑖𝑠 = ℎ𝑖𝑠 + 𝑒𝑠.  (7) 
The identity embedding in (7) is learned jointly with the 
encoder. It does not contain hand -coded gas knowledge. 
Instead, it gives the model a stable way to know which physical 
channel generated a token. This is a useful inductive bias for 
heterogeneous sensor arrays because channel identity is part of 
the sensing system itself. 
The sensor-ID mechanism is deliberately simple. It does not 
assume that one sensor is selective to one gas. It only preserves 
channel meaning and lets downstream attention learn how each 
sensor participates in the array response.

IV. DATASETS AND EXPERIMENT PROTOCOL 
A. NH3/H2 dataset 
The private  NH3/H2 dataset contains 162,404 sensor -
response samples grouped into 378 gas exposures.  Every 
measurement has 20 sensor -response channels, and the 
concentration range is 0 –500 ppm for both gases.  For the 
evaluation protocol, these exposures are divided into six non -
overlapping partitions of 63 exposures. Each partition contains 
57 unique (NH3,H2) concentration pairs and six repeated 
exposures.

Partitions 1–4 are augmented for unlabeled pretraining. Five 
64-sample windows are extracted from each exposure by 
shifting the starting offset from 0 to 4 time steps. Therefore, 
these four partitions contain 252 exposures and provide 1,260 
pretraining windows. Partition 5 is used for labeled adaptation 
and contains 63 exposures, including 57 candidate 
concentration pairs. Finally, partition 6 contains the remaining 
63 exposures and is reserved for testing without window 
augmentation. Only its offset -zero window is scored, so each 
test exposure contributes one prediction. 
Complete exposures are assigned to a partition before 
window extraction, and scaling parameters are fitted using 
partitions 1–4 only. Partition 6 remains excluded from scaling, 
pretraining, concentration-pair selection, supervised fitting, and 
checkpoint selection to prevent data leakage. 
B. Dynamic Gas Mixture Dataset 
The dynamic gas mixture dataset is publicly available from 
the UCI Machine Learning Repository [15], DOI 
10.24432/C5WP4C. Its acquisition and continuous -monitoring 
protocol are described in  [8]. This work uses the CO and 
ethylene portion, prepared as 245 chronological exposure runs 
with 16 response channels and 1,225 stored windows. Each run 
contributes five 64-sample windows at offsets 0, 20, 40, 60, and 
80. The processed target scale is 0 –533.33 ppm for CO and 0 –
20 ppm for ethylene. 
The 245 runs are divided, in acquisition order, into five 
partitions of 49 runs. Partitions 1–3 provide 147 runs and 735 
windows for unlabeled pretraining. Partition 4 provides 49 runs 
and 24 unique concentration pairs for labeled adaptation. 
Partition 5 provides 49 future runs for untouched evaluation. As 
in the NH3/H2 dataset, adaptation makes use of the data 
augmentation technique to create five windows from a selected 
exposure, whereas no data augmentation is applied for partition 
5. 
C. Sensor Drift Dataset 
To evaluate long -term drift, we use the public sensor drift 
dataset [16], DOI 10.24432/C5MK6M. The drift-compensation 
setting is described by Vergara et al. [17], and calibration with 
a limited number of experiments is examined in [14]. The 
dataset contain s 13,910 complete records organized into ten 
time-based partitions spanning months 1–36. Each record holds 
128 values: eight engineered descriptors for each of 16 sensor 
channels, so that each sample is a 16×8 matrix. The eight 
descriptors per sensor are two resistance-change features, three 
rising-response exponential -moving-average features, and 
three decaying-response exponential-moving-average features; 
they are engineered descriptors rather than consecutive 
temporal samples. The six gases are ethanol, ethylene, 
ammonia, acetaldehyde, acetone, and toluene. 
Partitions 1 –6, covering months 1 –20, are used for self -
supervised pretraining. Partition 7, collected in month 21, 
provides limited labeled adaptation data, and partitions 8 –10, 
covering months 22 –36, are retained as separate future tests. 
The adaptation budget is 20 selected records per gas; four 
records per gas are reserved for adaptation -only validation, 
leaving 16 per gas for supervised fitting in each repeat. 
Experiments use seeds 42, 142, and 242. 
Raw temporal curves are unavailable in this benchmark, so 
the self-supervised masking is adapted to the representation. A 
complete sensor is masked independently with probability 0.18. 
One descriptor group —the resistance pair, the rising 
descriptors, or the decaying descriptors—is selected per record 
and hidden independently on each sensor with probability 0.35, 
and individua l descriptors are additionally masked with 
probability 0.08. The shared principle remains structured 
reconstruction of physically grouped sensor information. The 
descriptor encoder maps the eight scaled descriptors and eight 
mask indicators through a share d 16→64→32 GELU 
multilayer perceptron, adds a learned 32 -dimensional sensor -
identity embedding, applies the same two -layer four -head 
Transformer, and pools mean and maximum across the 16 
contextual tokens followed by a 64 -unit GELU layer with 
dropout 0.15. 
The supervised objective for this extension contains only the 
gas-conditional concentration term. Six gas -conditional 
regression heads output normalized log -concentration values, 
and the known gas identity selects the corresponding output; no 
gas-classification head is trained or evaluated. The reported 
concentration MAE is therefore an oracle -class conditional 
metric: it assumes gas identity is available and does not 
represent end-to-end concentration prediction for an unknown 
gas. Per-gas concentrations are mapped to normalized log space 
as in (17).

D. Preprocessing and Optimization 
For the two raw-response datasets, a per-sensor robust scaler 
is fitted on unlabeled pretraining windows only. The center is 
the median and the scale is the interquartile range; scaled values 
are clipped to [−12,12]. Targets are divided by fixed 
implementation scales before Huber training: [500,500] ppm 
for the NH3/H2 dataset and [533.33,20] ppm for the dynamic 
gas mixture dataset. Predictions are transformed back to ppm 
and clipped to these ranges. 
Raw-response pretraining uses AdamW for 60 epochs with 
learning rate 8 × 10−4, weight decay 10−4, batch size 64, and 
gradient-norm clipping at 2. Supervised fitting uses 120 epochs 
and Huber loss with 𝛿 = 0.1 in normalized target space. Scratch 
training uses learning rate 5 × 10−4. Frozen transfer trains only 
the head at 10−3. Fine-tuning uses 8 × 10−5 for the encoder and 
8 × 10−4 for the head. All supervised policies use weight decay 
2 × 10−4 and batches of at most 64 samples.

V. RESULTS 
A. Self-Supervised Pretraining 
The reconstruction objective converges on all three data 
settings as shown in Fig.3 . For the NH3/H2 dataset, the 
reconstruction loss decreases from 2.1595 to 0.0762. For the 
dynamic gas mixture dataset, the loss reaches approximately 
0.165 at epoch 51, while the final stochastic -mask loss is 
0.3020. The difference is expected because a new random mask 
is generated over training and therefore the difficulty changes 
from epoch to epoch. The sensor drift dataset descriptor loss 
also falls steadily. Across three repeats, it moves from 0.1941 –
0.2118 initially to 0.0315–0.0321. 
Reconstruction loss alone is not evidence of a good 
calibration model. A network could reconstruct smooth or 
redundant signals without preserving the information needed 
for concentration estimation. We therefore use the downstream 
experiments as the main test of representation quality.

D. Multif-fold Validation 
To check whether improvement depends on one favorable 
partition, the NH3/H2 dataset is rerun in six leave -one-
partition-out folds. Each fold holds out one partition for testing, 
uses the preceding partition for adaptation (partition 6 when 
partition 1 is t ested), and uses the other four partitions for

scratch obtains 33.01±17.81 ppm NH3 MAE and 48.78±19.27

ppm for NH3, with a 95% bootstrap interval of 3.74–7.10 ppm,

gas contributes 1,134 paired predictions. 
For multi-fold validation, the dynamic gas mixture dataset is 
divided into seven smaller chronological partitions and

Ethylene improves by 0.386 ppm with an interval of 0.121 –
0.661 ppm. Each gas contributes 315 paired predict ions. All 
paired intervals remain above zero.

G. Long-Term Sensor Drift: Concentration Regression 
The sensor drift dataset contains six gases and spans 36 
months. Partitions 1 –6 are used for pretraining, partition 7 
supplies limited labeled adaptation data, and partitions 8–10 are 
held out as future drift conditions. When the known gas identity 
selects the corresponding concentration head, transfer improves 
macro-MAE in every future partition, as shown in Table VI. In 
partition 8, scratch achieves MAE of  23.14 ppm, while fine -
tuned transfer reaches 19.14 ppm, a 17.3% gain. In partition 9, 
fine-tuned transfer is best at 14.77 ppm compared with 21.06 
ppm for scratch, a 29.9% gain.  Partition 10 is much harder. 
Scratch MAE increases to 146.95 ppm. Frozen transfer reduces

H. Tail Error and Predictive Uncertainty 
Mean error can hide occasional large failures. We therefore 
also inspect the upper tail of absolute error. Transfer reduces 
large errors for NH3, H2, and CO. For ethylene, the tail 
behavior depends more strongly on the adaptation policy. This 
again supports the view that frozen and fine -tuned transfer are 
not interchangeable. 
We also use split -conformal prediction as a diagnostic. 
Nominal 90% coverage is not achieved under the temporal 
shifts. Empirical coverage is 81.3% for NH3, 71.5% for H2, 
59.0% for CO, and 63.8% for ethylene. These values are well 
below the nominal level, especially for the dynamic gas mixture 
dataset. 
Although point predictions improve, the intervals remain 
poorly calibrated after distribution shift. They also become 
wider and less stable when the calibration set is small. Thus, 
some form of shift -aware calibration would be useful before 
deployment. 
VI. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 
The encoder appears to learn more than a generic compression 
of the input. Sensor identity helps with several targets, which 
suggests that the physical channel index carries useful 
information. Complete-sensor masking is also beneficial in most 
cases, indicating that a missing response can be partly inferred 
from the remaining array. Temporal -span masking plays a 
different role because it encourages the encoder to follow local 
response evolution instead of relying only on a final value. In the 
ablation study, this is particularly useful for NH3 and H2. Cross-
sensor attention then combines the sensor tokens at array level, 
allowing the model to represent both within-sensor dynamics and 
relationships between sensors. At the same time, attention has a 
mixed effect for NH3, and no single component dominates every 
target. The observed gain therefore appears to come from the 
broader pretraining strategy rather than from one isolated layer. 
The advantage of transfer is strongest when only a few labeled 
concentration pairs are available. With five or ten pairs, a scratch 
model must learn sensor features and the calibration map at the 
same time. The transferred model already contains structure 
learned from unlabeled measurements, so the supervised stage 
begins with a smaller learning problem. 
The long-term drift experiment suggests that concentration -
related structure learned from earlier partitions remains useful 
later in time. Regression may depend on response magnitude, 
descriptor trends, or correlations within each gas. Some of these 
relationships appear to persist even as the overall sensor response 
changes, although the present experiments do not identify which 
mechanism is most important. The large absolute error observed 
in the last drift partition also shows that pretraining alone is not 
sufficient. An explicit temporal- or domain-alignment objective 
added after sensor-aware pretraining could improve robustness 
under stronger drift. 
The closest related studies already address quantitative gas 
prediction. Ni et al. [9] show that deep temporal-spatial feature 
fusion supports quantitative analysis, Tang et al.  [10] 
demonstrate transfer learning for a compact sensor system, Xia 
et al. [11] focus on small-data mixed-gas monitoring, and OT-
DARN [12] targets domain-adaptive concentration prediction. In 
our framework, pretraining requires no concentration labels and 
still preserves physical sensor identity. This makes it suitable for 
a sen sing system that accumulates unlabeled measurements 
before a smaller controlled calibration campaign is performed. 
However, previous studies use different datasets and evaluation 
splits, so to the best of the authors’ knowledge there is no suitable 
direct comparison between this work and previous studies. 
In summary, this work examines whether unlabeled Electronic 
nose measurements can reduce the amount of labeled data needed 
for quantitative calibration. To do so, we introduced a sensor -
aware self -supervised encoder with shared temporal feature 
extraction, physical sensor identity, cross-sensor attention, and 
structured masking. The underlying idea is straightforward: first 
learn recurring behavior in the sensor system, and then use 
labeled measurements to learn concentration. 
Across the NH3/H2 dataset and dynamic gas mixture dataset, 
transfer improves concentration regression for all four targets. 
The largest benefits appear when only a small number of labeled 
calibration pairs are available. Moreover, multi -fold validation 
shows paired improvement across held -out NH3/H2 partitions 
and future chronological partitions. 
On the 36-month sensor drift dataset, transfer improves oracle-
class gas -conditional concentration MAE in every future 
partition. The improvement is smaller in partition 10, where the 
absolute error remains high, but the representation still retains 
useful quantitative structure under this protocol.
