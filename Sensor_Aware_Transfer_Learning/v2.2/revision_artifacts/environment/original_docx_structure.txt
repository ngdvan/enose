P4 [Heading 1] Introduction
P5 [Text] E
P6 [PARA] lectronic nose systems infer gas properties from the combined response of partially selective sensors. For concentration estimation, they are normally calibrated through controlled exposures with known compositions. Collecting these labels becomes costly as the number of gases, mixtures, and measurement sessions grows. Moreover, aging sensors may require the calibration process to be repeated.
P7 [PARA] Meanwhile, unlabeled measurements are often available in much larger quantities. Although they do not provide direct concentration targets, they still contain temporal response shapes and recurring relationships among sensors. It is therefore reasonable to ask whether these patterns can be learned first and then reused when a smaller labeled calibration set becomes available.
P8 [PARA] Several earlier studies have already made use of limited or unlabeled Electronic nose data. M-Training [1] and M-S4VMs [2], for example, use pseudo-labeling for gas recognition. Drift research has taken other directions, including domain adaptation, few-shot learning, and meta-learning [3]–[7]. Quantitative calibration appears less often in this literature, even though it is important for monitoring applications.
P9 [PARA] There is also evidence that temporal responses contain useful concentration information. Reservoir computing has tracked changing concentrations despite slow sensor dynamics [8]. More recently, CNN-LSTM models and lightweight transferred networks have supported both recognition and concentration estimation [9], [10]. Small-data transfer and domain-adaptive regression offer further options when calibration data are limited [11], [12].
P10 [PARA] In this work, the sensor array is treated as more than an anonymous multivariate series. Each channel belongs to a physical sensor and may have its own response behavior. Accordingly, a shared temporal encoder learns common dynamics, while sensor identities and cross-sensor attention preserve the organization of the array. Pretraining hides time spans and complete channels, which must be recovered from the remaining measurements. The encoder is later frozen or cautiously fine-tuned for concentration regression. The study tests this approach on two raw-response datasets and across several label budgets. It also follows the public sensor drift dataset into future partitions. Transfer improves two-gas regression and oracle-class gas-conditional concentration estimates under drift. The complete pipeline is shown in Fig. 1.
P11 [Heading 1] Related Work
P12 [Normal] Quantitative estimation becomes important when an Electronic nose is used as a measuring instrument rather than only as a recognizer. Fonollosa et al. [8] used reservoir computing to estimate rapidly changing concentrations from slower sensor responses. Their study drew attention to transient dynamics, which may be lost when a response is reduced to a steady-state value.
P13 [Normal] Deep models have since combined recognition and regression in several ways. Ni et al. [9] used GAF-MTF representations with a CNN-LSTM model, reporting 95.31% classification accuracy and an R^2 of 0.9510. Tang et al. [10] instead paired a single MEMS sensor with a lightweight transferred network. Work on smaller datasets includes parameter-feature transfer [11], while OT-DARN uses teacher-student domain adaptation with selected pseudo-labels [12].
P14 [Normal] M-Training [1] and M-S4VMs [2] bring unlabeled samples into Electronic nose recognition through predicted labels. This strategy can be helpful when labels are scarce, although incorrect predictions may be reinforced as training continues.
P15 [Normal] Self-supervised learning avoids that particular dependence by taking its pretraining target from the measurements themselves. Common time-series objectives include masking, reconstruction, contrastive learning, and adversarial learning [13]. Masked reconstruction is a natural fit for sensor arrays because missing intervals require temporal context. Likewise, missing channels encourage the model to use information shared across sensors. CEGNN [6] takes a different route by combining contrastive learning, graph reasoning, and a small reference set.
P16 [Normal]  In addition to accurately estimating gas concentrations, sensor drift due to aging is also a challenging problem. Sensor drift gradually changes the response distribution, so models trained at one time may not behave in the same way later. TDACNN [3] addresses source-target differences, whereas SAD-CNN [5] combines a one-dimensional CNN with adversarial adaptation and pseudo-labeling. MDCN [4] uses metric learning with a small reference set. Other studies extend the setting through contrastive graph reasoning [6] or model-agnostic meta-learning [7], including tests involving class asymmetry, different devices, and future drift. Most evaluations on the 36-month benchmark emphasize whether gas identity survives the shift, while concentration behavior receives less attention. For this reason, we use its future partitions to study oracle-class gas-conditional concentration regression under temporal drift.
P17 [Normal] Overall, these studies leave unlabeled representation learning and quantitative calibration only partly connected. The present study bridges that gap by first learning sensor-specific temporal and cross-channel structure without concentration labels, and then reusing the encoder with a small labeled calibration set. The future-partition evaluation further examines whether this quantitative transfer remains useful as the sensors drift, and where further adaptation is needed. The proposed approach is expected to reduce the labeled-calibration effort for electronic nose systems, and the drift evaluation tests how far the self-supervised representation stays useful as the sensors age.

P72 [Heading 1] Datasets and Experiment Protocol
P73 [Heading 2] NH3/H2 dataset
P74 [PARA_Indent] The private NH3/H2 dataset contains 162,404 sensor-response samples grouped into 378 gas exposures. Every measurement has 20 sensor-response channels, and the concentration range is 0–500 ppm for both gases. For the evaluation protocol, these exposures are divided into six non-overlapping partitions of 63 exposures. Each partition contains 57 unique (NH3,H2) concentration pairs and six repeated exposures. 
P75 [PARA_Indent] Partitions 1–4 are augmented for unlabeled pretraining. Five 64-sample windows are extracted from each exposure by shifting the starting offset from 0 to 4 time steps. Therefore, these four partitions contain 252 exposures and provide 1,260 pretraining windows. Partition 5 is used for labeled adaptation and contains 63 exposures, including 57 candidate concentration pairs. Finally, partition 6 contains the remaining 63 exposures and is reserved for testing without window augmentation. Only its offset-zero window is scored, so each test exposure contributes one prediction.
P76 [PARA_Indent] Complete exposures are assigned to a partition before window extraction, and scaling parameters are fitted using partitions 1–4 only. Partition 6 remains excluded from scaling, pretraining, concentration-pair selection, supervised fitting, and checkpoint selection to prevent data leakage.
P77 [Heading 2] Dynamic Gas Mixture Dataset
P78 [PARA_Indent] The dynamic gas mixture dataset is publicly available from the UCI Machine Learning Repository [15], DOI 10.24432/C5WP4C. Its acquisition and continuous-monitoring protocol are described in  [8]. This work uses the CO and ethylene portion, prepared as 245 chronological exposure runs with 16 response channels and 1,225 stored windows. Each run contributes five 64-sample windows at offsets 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80. The processed target scale is 0–533.33 ppm for CO and 0–20 ppm for ethylene.
P79 [PARA_Indent] The 245 runs are divided, in acquisition order, into five partitions of 49 runs. Partitions 1–3 provide 147 runs and 735 windows for unlabeled pretraining. Partition 4 provides 49 runs and 24 unique concentration pairs for labeled adaptation. Partition 5 provides 49 future runs for untouched evaluation. As in the NH3/H2 dataset, adaptation makes use of the data augmentation technique to create five windows from a selected exposure, whereas no data augmentation is applied for partition 5.
P80 [Heading 2] Sensor Drift Dataset
P81 [PARA_Indent] To evaluate long-term drift, we use the public sensor drift dataset [16], DOI 10.24432/C5MK6M. The drift-compensation setting is described by Vergara et al. [17], and calibration with a limited number of experiments is examined in [14]. The dataset contains 13,910 complete records organized into ten time-based partitions spanning months 1–36. Each record holds 128 values: eight engineered descriptors for each of 16 sensor channels, so that each sample is a 16×8 matrix. The eight descriptors per sensor are two resistance-change features, three rising-response exponential-moving-average features, and three decaying-response exponential-moving-average features; they are engineered descriptors rather than consecutive temporal samples. The six gases are ethanol, ethylene, ammonia, acetaldehyde, acetone, and toluene.
P82 [PARA_Indent] Partitions 1–6, covering months 1–20, are used for self-supervised pretraining. Partition 7, collected in month 21, provides limited labeled adaptation data, and partitions 8–10, covering months 22–36, are retained as separate future tests. The adaptation budget is 20 selected records per gas; four records per gas are reserved for adaptation-only validation, leaving 16 per gas for supervised fitting in each repeat. Experiments use seeds 42, 142, and 242.
P83 [PARA_Indent] Raw temporal curves are unavailable in this benchmark, so the self-supervised masking is adapted to the representation. A complete sensor is masked independently with probability 0.18. One descriptor group—the resistance pair, the rising descriptors, or the decaying descriptors—is selected per record and hidden independently on each sensor with probability 0.35, and individual descriptors are additionally masked with probability 0.08. The shared principle remains structured reconstruction of physically grouped sensor information. The descriptor encoder maps the eight scaled descriptors and eight mask indicators through a shared 16→64→32 GELU multilayer perceptron, adds a learned 32-dimensional sensor-identity embedding, applies the same two-layer four-head Transformer, and pools mean and maximum across the 16 contextual tokens followed by a 64-unit GELU layer with dropout 0.15.
P84 [PARA_Indent] The supervised objective for this extension contains only the gas-conditional concentration term. Six gas-conditional regression heads output normalized log-concentration values, and the known gas identity selects the corresponding output; no gas-classification head is trained or evaluated. The reported concentration MAE is therefore an oracle-class conditional metric: it assumes gas identity is available and does not represent end-to-end concentration prediction for an unknown gas. Per-gas concentrations are mapped to normalized log space as in (17).

P133 [Heading 2] Tail Error and Predictive Uncertainty
P134 [Normal] Mean error can hide occasional large failures. We therefore also inspect the upper tail of absolute error. Transfer reduces large errors for NH3, H2, and CO. For ethylene, the tail behavior depends more strongly on the adaptation policy. This again supports the view that frozen and fine-tuned transfer are not interchangeable.
P135 [Normal] We also use split-conformal prediction as a diagnostic. Nominal 90% coverage is not achieved under the temporal shifts. Empirical coverage is 81.3% for NH3, 71.5% for H2, 59.0% for CO, and 63.8% for ethylene. These values are well below the nominal level, especially for the dynamic gas mixture dataset.
P136 [Normal] Although point predictions improve, the intervals remain poorly calibrated after distribution shift. They also become wider and less stable when the calibration set is small. Thus, some form of shift-aware calibration would be useful before deployment.
P137 [Heading 1] Discussion And Conclusion
P138 [PARA_Indent] The encoder appears to learn more than a generic compression of the input. Sensor identity helps with several targets, which suggests that the physical channel index carries useful information. Complete-sensor masking is also beneficial in most cases, indicating that a missing response can be partly inferred from the remaining array. Temporal-span masking plays a different role because it encourages the encoder to follow local response evolution instead of relying only on a final value. In the ablation study, this is particularly useful for NH3 and H2. Cross-sensor attention then combines the sensor tokens at array level, allowing the model to represent both within-sensor dynamics and relationships between sensors. At the same time, attention has a mixed effect for NH3, and no single component dominates every target. The observed gain therefore appears to come from the broader pretraining strategy rather than from one isolated layer.
P139 [PARA_Indent] The advantage of transfer is strongest when only a few labeled concentration pairs are available. With five or ten pairs, a scratch model must learn sensor features and the calibration map at the same time. The transferred model already contains structure learned from unlabeled measurements, so the supervised stage begins with a smaller learning problem.
P140 [PARA_Indent] The long-term drift experiment suggests that concentration-related structure learned from earlier partitions remains useful later in time. Regression may depend on response magnitude, descriptor trends, or correlations within each gas. Some of these relationships appear to persist even as the overall sensor response changes, although the present experiments do not identify which mechanism is most important. The large absolute error observed in the last drift partition also shows that pretraining alone is not sufficient. An explicit temporal- or domain-alignment objective added after sensor-aware pretraining could improve robustness under stronger drift.
P141 [PARA_Indent] The closest related studies already address quantitative gas prediction. Ni et al. [9] show that deep temporal-spatial feature fusion supports quantitative analysis, Tang et al. [10] demonstrate transfer learning for a compact sensor system, Xia et al. [11] focus on small-data mixed-gas monitoring, and OT-DARN [12] targets domain-adaptive concentration prediction. In our framework, pretraining requires no concentration labels and still preserves physical sensor identity. This makes it suitable for a sensing system that accumulates unlabeled measurements before a smaller controlled calibration campaign is performed. However, previous studies use different datasets and evaluation splits, so to the best of the authors’ knowledge there is no suitable direct comparison between this work and previous studies.
P142 [PARA_Indent] In summary, this work examines whether unlabeled Electronic nose measurements can reduce the amount of labeled data needed for quantitative calibration. To do so, we introduced a sensor-aware self-supervised encoder with shared temporal feature extraction, physical sensor identity, cross-sensor attention, and structured masking. The underlying idea is straightforward: first learn recurring behavior in the sensor system, and then use labeled measurements to learn concentration.
P143 [PARA_Indent] Across the NH3/H2 dataset and dynamic gas mixture dataset, transfer improves concentration regression for all four targets. The largest benefits appear when only a small number of labeled calibration pairs are available. Moreover, multi-fold validation shows paired improvement across held-out NH3/H2 partitions and future chronological partitions.
P144 [PARA_Indent] On the 36-month sensor drift dataset, transfer improves oracle-class gas-conditional concentration MAE in every future partition. The improvement is smaller in partition 10, where the absolute error remains high, but the representation still retains useful quantitative structure under this protocol.

Abstract—  Electronic nose  calibration usually relies on controlled gas exposures with known concentrations. In practice, however, unlabeled responses are much easier to collect because they carry no concentration annotation. The present experiments reuse controlled exposures with their concentration labels withheld during pretraining, so they demonstrate reduced labeled-calibration requirements rather than reduced gas-generation cost. This study explores whether those unlabeled measurements can be useful before the calibration stage through sensor-aware self-supervised learning. The proposed model uses a shared dilated one-dimensional convolutional encoder to learn temporal behavior. Sensor embeddings preserve channel identity, while attention captures relationships across the array. During pretraining, the model reconstructs masked time spans and complete sensor channels. The resulting encoder is then adapted using a limited number of concentration labels. The evaluation includes a private 20-channel NH3/H2 array, a public 16-channel CO/ethylene array, and a public 36-month sensor drift dataset. At the full label budget, transfer achieves mean absolute errors of 14.92 ppm for NH3, 29.16 ppm for H2, 46.16 ppm for CO, and 1.371 ppm for ethylene. These results correspond to gains of 19.2%, 20.3%, 29.0%, and 31.2% over scratch training. The benefit generally becomes larger when fewer labels are available. On sensor drift dataset, oracle-class gas-conditional concentration error decreases in three future partitions by 17.3%, 29.9%, and 2.8%. Quantitative transfer therefore retains some within-gas concentration information under long-term drift, although the benefit decreases under the strongest future shift.
Index Terms— Electronic nose, self-supervised learning, transfer learning, gas concentration regression, sensor drift, label-efficient calibration, sensor attention.
