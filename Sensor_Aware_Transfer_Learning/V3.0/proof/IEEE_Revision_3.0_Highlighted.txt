II. RELATED WORK
Fonollosa et al. [8] used reservoir computing to recover 
rapidly changing concentrations from slow sensor responses, 
demonstrating transient information beyond steady-state 
features.
Quantitative models include GAF-MTF/CNN-LSTM feature 
fusion [9], a lightweight transferred network with a single 
MEMS sensor [10], small-data parameter-feature transfer [11], 
and teacher-student domain-adaptive regression [12].
M-Training [1] and M-S4VMs [2] use predicted labels for 
gas recognition, with potential reinforcement of incorrect 
pseudo-labels.
Self-supervision instead derives targets from measurements. 
Time-series objectives include reconstruction, masking, 
contrastive and adversarial learning [13]. Masking intervals and 
channels encourages temporal and cross-sensor inference 
without concentration pseudo-labels.
Self-Supervised Transfer Learning for Label-
Efficient Multigas Electronic Nose Calibration
Dinh-Van Nguyen, DangThiThu Ha, Thu-Huong Nguyen , and NguyenDuc Hoa
Abstract— Self-supervised transfer reduces mean absolute concentration error relative to training from scratch by 
13.9% for NH3, 21.5% for H2, 27.5% for CO, and 25.7% for ethylene in our primary multigas electronic-nose evaluation. 
We pretrain a shared temporal encoder using masked sensor-response reconstruction, then fine-tune it with a limited 
set of labeled concentration pairs. The evaluation uses ten seeds, six held-out identifier partitions for NH3/H2, and three 
rolling chronological folds for CO/ethylene; the NH3/H2 results use a predefined early-response window. In secondary 
fixed-split experiments, fine-tuning with 10 labeled pairs gives approximately the NH3 and ethylene errors of scratch 
training with 20 pairs, a lower mean CO error, and a higher H2 error. These pair counts include validation labels. Sensor-
identity and cross-sensor-attention ablations show target-dependent effects, with NH3 the weakest transfer case. In a 
known-gas-identity drift benchmark, transfer improves concentration regression in partitions 8 and 9 but not clearly in 
partition 10. The proposed model is not the best full-budget baseline on any of the four raw-response targets. The 
findings support target-dependent label efficiency for same-system calibration; the NH3/H2 identifier partitions do not 
establish between-day generalization.
Index Terms— Electronic nose, self-supervised learning, transfer learning, gas concentration regression, sensor drift, 
label-efficient calibration, sensor attention.

The implemented temporal encoder receives two channels

The jointly learned embedding in (7) preserves physical

Sensor identities encode channel correspondence, not one-
sensor/one-gas selectivity. Experiments reuse the same array 
within each dataset and do not establish cross-device transfer.

IV.
DATASETS AND EXPERIMENT PROTOCOL
A.NH3/H2 dataset
The NH3/H2 dataset [29] contains 162,404 observations 
from 20 Nano MEMS sensor channels, grouped into 378 
recorded sequences, with concentrations of 0–500 ppm for each 
gas. Acquisition took place over six days. The six partitions of 
63 sequences were constructed from consecutive Combination 
identifiers and were not designed as day splits. Day-by-day 
mixture details were not logged, so a reliable exposure-to-day 
mapping and leave-one-day-out evaluation cannot be 
reconstructed. Training and test partitions may share 
acquisition days; these folds do not establish between-day 
generalization.
The primary NH3/H2 evaluation holds out each of the six 
partitions in turn, uses the preceding cyclic partition for 
adaptation, and uses the remaining four for pretraining. Because 
some pretraining partitions follow the test partition, this is held-
partition validation, not prospective forecasting. The secondary 
fixed split uses partitions 1–4 for pretraining (252 exposures; 
1,260 windows), partition 5 for adaptation, and partition 6 for 
testing. Five 64-sample training windows use offsets 0–4; tests 
use offset zero only. Related image-based mixed-gas estimation 
is described by Ha et al. [30].
Within every fold, complete exposures are assigned before 
window extraction. Response scaling is fitted exclusively to 
that fold’s pretraining responses. Adaptation fitting and 
validation exposures are disjoint, and all policies use identical 
group assignments within each seed and budget. Test exposures 
are excluded from preprocessing fits, pretraining, calibration 
design, fitting, and checkpoint selection. A budget counts both 
fitting and validation concentration pairs.
B.Dynamic Gas Mixture Dataset
The dynamic gas mixture dataset is publicly available from 
the UCI Machine Learning Repository [15]. Its acquisition and 
continuous-monitoring protocol are described in  [8]. This work 
uses the CO and ethylene portion, prepared as 245 
chronological exposure runs with 16 response channels and 
1,225 stored windows. Each run contributes five 64-sample 
windows at offsets 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80. The processed target 
scale is 0–533.33 ppm for CO and 0–20 ppm for ethylene.
Primary CO/ethylene validation divides the 245 acquisition-
ordered groups into seven blocks and evaluates three rolling 
folds, each with three pretraining blocks followed by one 
adaptation block and one future test block. Secondary fixed-
split experiments retain five blocks of 49 groups: blocks 1–3 
pretrain, block 4 adapts, and block 5 tests. Budgets are 5, 10, 20,

and 24 unique concentration pairs. Training uses five stored 
offsets; the test uses one window per group.
C.Sensor Drift Dataset
To evaluate long-term drift, we use the public sensor drift 
dataset [16]. The drift-compensation setting is described by 
Vergara et al. [17], and calibration with a limited number of 
experiments is examined in [14]. The dataset contains 13,910 
complete records organized into ten time-based partitions 
spanning months 1–36. Each record holds 128 values: eight 
engineered descriptors for each of 16 sensor channels, so that 
each sample is a 16×8 matrix. The eight descriptors per sensor 
are two resistance-change features, three rising-response 
exponential-moving-average features, and three decaying-
response exponential-moving-average features; they are 
engineered descriptors rather than consecutive temporal 
samples. The six gases are ethanol, ethylene, ammonia, 
acetaldehyde, acetone, and toluene.
Partitions 1–6 (months 1–20) pretrain the descriptor encoder; 
partition 7 (month 21) provides adaptation labels; partitions 8–
10 are separate future tests. We evaluate 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 
labels per gas with 10 seeds. At 20 labels/gas, 16 fit and four 
validate each gas, identically across policies. One label/gas has 
no disjoint holdout and therefore uses the predefined final 
epoch; larger budgets select checkpoints using adaptation-only 
validation.
Raw temporal curves are unavailable in this benchmark, so 
the self-supervised masking is adapted to the representation. A 
complete sensor is masked independently with probability 0.18. 
One descriptor group—the resistance pair, the rising 
descriptors, or the decaying descriptors—is selected per record 
and hidden independently on each sensor with probability 0.35, 
and individual descriptors are additionally masked with 
probability 0.08. The shared principle remains structured 
reconstruction of physically grouped sensor information. The 
descriptor encoder maps the eight scaled descriptors and eight 
mask indicators through a shared 16→64→32 GELU 
multilayer perceptron, adds a learned 32-dimensional sensor-
identity embedding, applies the same two-layer four-head 
Transformer, and pools mean and maximum across the 16 
contextual tokens followed by a 64-unit GELU layer with 
dropout 0.15.
The supervised objective for this extension contains only the 
gas-conditional concentration term. Six gas-conditional 
regression heads output normalized log-concentration values, 
and the known gas identity selects the corresponding output; no 
gas-classification head is trained or evaluated. The reported 
concentration MAE is therefore an oracle-class conditional 
metric: it assumes gas identity is available and does not 
represent end-to-end concentration prediction for an unknown 
gas. The predefined 20-label analysis uses the normalized log 
targets in (17); fixed-scale log and linear losses are additional 
sensitivity controls.

The generic SSL control uses eight nonoverlapping length-8 
patches per channel, 32-dimensional tokens, two four-head 
Transformer layers, 96-unit feed-forward blocks, shared 
channel weights, temporal positions, and three masked patches 
per channel. It has no physical sensor embeddings or cross-
channel attention. Temporal mean pooling feeds the same 
concatenated-channel regression head, so channel ordering 
remains available. Instance normalization is omitted to preserve 
absolute responses and match preprocessing; capacity differs 
and is reported. Pretraining/adaptation data, budgets, seeds, 
scaling and checkpoint rules match the proposed model.
Classical regressors receive flattened pretraining-scaled 
windows: 1,280 NH3/H2 or 1,024 CO/ethylene features. PLSR 
and separate-target RBF SVR additionally standardize features 
using fitting data only. Hyperparameters are selected by 
adaptation-validation normalized MAE. Tree settings are fixed. 
At 20 drift labels/gas, historical log-Huber is compared with

a standard deviation from tiny fitting sets. The 1000-ppm 
divisor is a numerical unit, not an asserted physical maximum. 
Constant controls use only the per-gas fitting mean.

VI.
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Masked self-supervised pretraining reduces mean error 
against scratch in this same-system calibration evaluation. 
Under predefined fine-tuning, primary reductions are 13.9% for 
NH3, 21.5% for H2, 27.5% for CO and 25.7% for ethylene; 
NH3/H2 uses the early-response window. Fixed-split pair-count 
comparisons show approximately comparable NH3/ethylene 

IEEE SENSORS JOURNAL — REVISED MANUSCRIPT  |  9
Manuscript under review.
errors and lower mean CO error with 10 transfer pairs versus 20 
scratch pairs, but not an H2 match at those budgets.
The proposed model is not the best full-budget baseline on 
any of the four targets; CO MAE is 1.86 times that of PLSR. 
Sensor identity and attention are not uniformly beneficial, 
particularly for NH3. NH3 also has negligible five-pair 
improvement and is the weakest multiplicity-adjusted primary 
case. Window and calibration-design effects vary with target 
and budget. Remaining absolute and tail errors limit 
quantitative usefulness.
Oracle-class drift regression improves in partitions 8-9 but 
not clearly in partition 10. The evaluation assumes known gas 
identity. Constant, normalized-error and loss controls show that 
representation reuse alone does not establish severe-drift 
robustness.
Ten seeds quantify training/design variability, not 
independent acquisitions. NH3’s four-comparison adjusted 
interval includes zero; CO/ethylene intervals remain indicative 
with only three folds. NH3/H2 acquisition spanned six days, but 
missing day-by-day mixture logs prevent reliable day-held-out 
evaluation. Temperature and humidity were neither controlled 
nor measured. Consequently these identifier folds cannot 
establish between-day or environmental robustness. Cross-
device transfer remains unevaluated.
Label withholding tests the learning premise retrospectively, 
not acquisition-cost savings. Known-coordinate calibration 
design excludes candidate-pool quantification costs. Future 
experiments will measure and record environmental conditions; 
exposure-linked day/session logs are needed for independent-
day evaluation. Prospective unquantified-mixture pretraining, 
acquisition-cost accounting, and shift-aware uncertainty remain 
future work.
